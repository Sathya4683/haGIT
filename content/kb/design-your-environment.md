# Design your environment

When we fail at a habit, we usually blame motivation. Research on habits points somewhere else: the environment. Habits are responses triggered by the context we are in, which means changing the context changes the behaviour.

## Habits respond to cues

Psychologists Wendy Wood and David Neal describe habits as associations between a context (a place, a time, an object, a preceding action) and a response. Once a habit is strong, the cue can trigger the behaviour with little conscious intention. That is great for good habits and a problem for unwanted ones: the phone on your desk triggers scrolling, whether or not you want to scroll.

## Two levers: visibility and friction

**1. Make the cue for good habits obvious.**
- Leave your guitar on a stand in the middle of the room, not in its case.
- Put the book on your pillow.
- Keep a water bottle on your desk.
- Open your language app's widget on your home screen.

**2. Adjust the effort (friction).**
Small amounts of friction have outsized effects. Wood and Neal highlight that making a behaviour slightly more or less convenient can shift what people do, often more than information or encouragement.

- **Reduce friction for good habits:** lay out workout clothes the night before; keep healthy snacks pre-cut; bookmark the practice page.
- **Add friction to bad habits:** log out of social apps after each use; keep the TV remote in a drawer; charge your phone outside the bedroom.

## Disrupt old cues when you want to change

Big context changes (moving house, starting a new job, a new semester) temporarily break old cues. Research on habit discontinuity suggests these moments are good windows for building new routines, because old habits lose their automatic triggers. If you can't move, create small disruptions: rearrange your desk, change your route, use a different room for focused work.

## Stable context builds new habits

The other side of the same principle: perform a new habit in the **same place, at the same time, after the same preceding action** as often as possible. A consistent context gives your brain a reliable cue to learn. (See *Same time, same place*.)

## A quick environment audit

1. What triggers your unwanted habit? Remove or hide it.
2. What would trigger the habit you want? Put it in your path.
3. What is the first physical step of the good habit? Prepare it in advance.
4. What is the first step of the bad habit? Add one more step to it.

## Key takeaway

Willpower is a weak lever; the environment is a strong one. Make good cues visible, cut the steps to start, and put a little friction between you and the habits you want to drop.
