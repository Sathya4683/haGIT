# Never miss twice

You had a perfect week, then life happened and you missed a day. It is tempting to think the habit is "broken" and you have to start over. The evidence says otherwise.

## What the research says about a single miss

In the University College London habit study (Lally and colleagues, 2010), participants did not perform their new behaviour every single day. The researchers found that **missing one opportunity did not materially affect the habit formation process**. Automaticity kept building as long as people returned to the behaviour.

Habits are built by the overall pattern of repetition in a consistent context, not by an unbroken chain. One gap is a blip in the curve, not a reset to zero.

## The real danger: the second miss

The risk is not the first missed day; it is what it can turn into. One miss makes the next miss easier ("I already broke it, so why bother?"). Two misses start to feel like the new normal. A week later, the habit is gone.

That is why many habit coaches use a simple rule, popularised by James Clear: **never miss twice**. Missing once is an accident. Missing twice is the start of a new pattern.

## How to get back on track

- **Do the smallest version today.** If your habit is "run 5 km", today it is "walk for 10 minutes". The goal is to restore the pattern, not to make up for lost time.
- **Don't double up.** Trying to compensate ("two workouts tomorrow!") raises the bar and makes the next miss more likely.
- **Find the cause, not a culprit.** Was it travel? A late meeting? A cue that disappeared? Adjust the plan with an if-then rule: "If I'm travelling, then I'll do 10 minutes of stretching in the hotel room."
- **Talk to yourself like a friend.** Self-criticism after a lapse tends to make people disengage. Treat it as information, not a verdict.

## Streaks without the all-or-nothing trap

Streaks are motivating, and research on streak displays shows people work to keep them alive. But a broken streak can feel like a loss that makes people quit. Keep an eye on your *longest* streak and your *overall consistency*, not just today's count. A month with 26 of 30 days committed is a very strong month.

One missed day doesn't undo your progress. Make the next day count, even in its smallest form, and never let one miss become two.
