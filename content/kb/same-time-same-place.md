# Same time, same place

In diary studies where people reported what they were doing every hour, psychologists Wendy Wood, Jeffrey Quinn and Deborah Kashy found that roughly **43% of everyday actions** were performed almost daily and usually in the same context. During those actions, people's minds were often elsewhere. The behaviour was running on autopilot, prompted by the setting.

That autopilot is exactly what you want for a new habit. The way to get there is to keep the context stable.

## Why consistency of context matters

Habits form when a behaviour is repeatedly paired with the same cues. If you exercise at 7 am on Monday, 9 pm on Tuesday and lunchtime on Thursday, your brain has no single cue to associate with exercise. If you exercise every weekday right after work, "leaving work" starts to trigger "gym".

A study of new gym members by Navin Kaushal and Ryan Rhodes found that those who built an exercise habit typically worked out **at least four times a week for about six weeks**, and having consistent cues for exercise was part of what predicted the habit forming.

## What counts as context

- **Time:** a clock time or, better, a point in your routine ("after dinner").
- **Place:** the same desk, room or route.
- **Preceding action:** the thing you just finished (see *Habit stacking*).
- **People and objects:** the same playlist, the same mug, the same study partner.

Preceding actions are often the most reliable anchors because they travel with you. "After my first coffee" works at home and on holiday.

## Practical tips

- **Pick one primary slot** for each habit and protect it like a meeting.
- **Keep a backup slot** for days the primary one is impossible.
- **Use the same setup each time:** same corner, same headphones, same opening ritual.
- **Look at your data.** haGIT's coach can tell you your typical check-in hour and strongest weekday. If you usually commit around 21:00, that is your natural slot; build around it.

## When your schedule isn't regular

Shift workers and students with changing timetables can still anchor to routine-based cues instead of clock times: "after I wake up", "when I get home", "before my first study session". The cue doesn't need to be at the same hour; it needs to be the same moment in your sequence.

## Key takeaway

Repeat your habit in the same context as often as you can. The more stable the cue, the sooner the behaviour stops needing a decision and starts happening on its own.
