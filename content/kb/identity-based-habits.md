# Identity-based habits

"I'm trying to run more" and "I'm a runner" describe the same plan, but they feel different. One is a chore you might skip; the other is part of who you are.

## The research, honestly

In a well-known 2011 study, Christopher Bryan and colleagues asked people about voting using either a verb ("How important is it to you to *vote*?") or a noun ("How important is it to you to *be a voter*?"). The noun version, which invites people to see voting as part of their identity, increased interest in registering and, in two follow-up studies, actual turnout measured from official records.

However, a large later field experiment by Alan Gerber and colleagues did **not** find the same turnout effect in a different setting. So identity framing is a promising idea with mixed evidence, not a guaranteed lever. It is best used together with concrete strategies like if-then plans and a stable context.

## Why identity can still help

- **Consistency pressure works in your favour.** People like to act in line with how they see themselves.
- **It survives bad days better.** A goal ("run a 10k") ends when you reach or abandon it; an identity ("I'm someone who moves every day") keeps going.
- **Every repetition is evidence.** Each time you do the habit, you collect proof of the identity, and the identity gets more believable.

## Putting it into practice

1. **Name the identity.** Not "lose 5 kg" but "I'm someone who takes care of my body". Not "finish the course" but "I'm a learner".
2. **Ask the identity question.** When deciding, ask: "What would a reader / runner / writer do right now?" Often the answer is the tiny version: read one page.
3. **Collect votes.** Treat each commit as a vote for the person you're becoming. Your contribution graph is a ballot box.
4. **Use identity words in your habits.** Branch names like `writer-daily` or commit messages like "showed up as a runner" are small but they reinforce the frame.

## Avoid the trap

Identity shouldn't become a reason for guilt ("a real runner wouldn't skip"). If a day goes wrong, an identity-based response is "runners have off days; I'll run tomorrow", not "I guess I'm not a runner".

## Key takeaway

Try framing your habits as who you are, not just what you do, and let every repetition count as evidence. Pair it with practical tactics, since identity framing alone has mixed research support.
